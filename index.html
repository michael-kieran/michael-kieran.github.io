About

A physics grad who became an infrastructure storyteller.

I studied physics and astronomy at the University of Toronto, then spent two decades marketing enterprise infrastructure — including years on the technical side as a technical marketing engineer at NetApp, writing the content that helped buyers, sellers, SEs, and channel partners understand storage area networks. That grounding still shapes how I work: I don't message a product until I understand how it actually behaves.

Today I lead messaging and go-to-market for IBM Storage Scale, one of the world's leading file and object storage platforms for HPC and AI workloads, as part of the IBM Fusion AI team. I own the marketing relationship between IBM Storage and NVIDIA, and I work with IBM Storage CTO Vincent Hsu on thought leadership around KV cache and inference memory — positioning KV cache as AI-native infrastructure.

What I enjoy most is translating complex technology into stories that resonate — whether the audience is a CIO, a channel partner, an industry analyst, or someone looking at a photo of a nebula for the first time.

Astrophotography

Away from work, I put my physics and astronomy training to use photographing deep-sky objects — and writing about the astrophysics of what's in the frame. It's the same craft as my day job: making something complex and invisible understandable. Click any image to view it full-screen.

Ghost Nebula — Sh2-136

The Ghost Nebula is a faint, eerie cloud of dust in the constellation Cepheus, about 1,400 light-years away. A fitting subject for October, the Ghost is well placed high in the northern sky on autumn evenings.

Unlike glowing emission nebulae that produce their own light, the Ghost is a reflection nebula. It contains a few young stars, but they're not hot enough to ionize the surrounding gas. As a result, the Ghost shines by starlight reflected off tiny dust grains, giving it a dim, brownish-gray, smoky appearance. It's captured here through a luminance filter, which records the full range of visible light as shades of gray.

The dust grains are made of silicates, carbon, and ice, and are about the size of the particles in cigarette smoke. Beyond its spooky looks, the Ghost Nebula is scientifically interesting because it's a stellar nursery. Its dense, dark core is a Bok globule, a collapsing pocket of gas and dust where new stars are forming. Longer exposures reveal jets from an embedded protostar — literally a star in the process of being born.
